# Account checks

Run these on **every** account before it goes in the course. Keep a short log of what each check caught: the traps become lesson 2's callouts, and they are the part founders remember.

| # | Check | How | The trap it catches |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The domain is real and is this company** | Fetch the homepage and follow redirects: a redirect after a rename is fine, a lookalike domain is not. A bot wall (403/503 challenge page) is not a dead site: confirm with the company's own releases or filings and mark `✓ domain (bot wall)` | A dead site, a parked domain, or a different business on that domain |
| 2 | **Not a name collision** | If the name is generic (Summit, Premier, Advanced, Atlas, Nova, Apex), confirm with domain plus location plus industry. Search the name with the city | Two companies with the same name in different states, or a near-identical name in the same industry ("Example Medical" vs "Example Medical Supply") |
| 3 | **Independent, or owned by someone** | Search "<company> acquired" and "<company> parent company". Check the site footer and the About page for "a <Group> company". Read the **newest** news first: a homepage can still say "family owned" years after a private-equity deal | A local brand that is really one location of a national chain, so the decision is made at head office, not here. In fragmented markets this is the most common trap by far |
| 4 | **Still operating** | The standard test: a careers page with posts from the last 60 days, plus one "<company> acquired" search, newest first. Public job boards give dated posts and make this fast. An official public registry for the industry also works (for US medical suppliers, the Medicare supplier directory shows new branches). A "© 2026" footer is **not** evidence: many sites print the current year automatically | A closed or merged business still showing in old directories |
| 5 | **Not an existing customer** | Compare against the named customers you collected. Former customers of a business line the company has sold now belong to the buyer: treat them as the buyer's customers | Pitching a company that already pays them, which is embarrassing on a demo |
| 6 | **Not a competitor or partner** | Compare against competitors and partners you collected | A company that sells the same thing, or resells it |
| 6b | **Still available** | Is what you want from them already taken? For a product: a competitor's tool is visible in public repos, integrations or trust pages, or job posts. For licensing or partnerships: the rights are already licensed to someone else in that territory | An account that has already bought the rival product, or an asset already licensed away |
| 6c | **Not a competitor's named customer** | Check competitors' case studies and logo walls | A company a rival is showcasing. Default: drop it from the first list and keep it on a watch list for when the contract comes up |
| 7 | **Fits the ICP on the facts** | Size, segment and region from the company's own site, not a guess | A company that looks right by name but is the wrong size or model |

## Rules

- **When a check can't be settled, drop the account.** Don't keep it with a question mark.
- Put the domain in the table as a link, so the founder can check it in one click.
- Record the result in the table's **Check** column: `✓ domain, location`, `✓ independent`, and so on.
- The account list is better at 25 clean rows than 40 with three wrong ones.

## Turning traps into teaching

For each trap caught, write a `callout` with `tone: "trap"`:

> **Two companies, one name.** Two freight brokers are both called "Examplefield Logistics", one in Ohio and one in Arizona. Only the Ohio one fits the size band. The domain settled it.

(That one is made up. Yours must be a real case from this run, with its source.) Real, specific and short. The course is more convincing with one of these than with ten more rows.

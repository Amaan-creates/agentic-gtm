# GTM principles

Each lesson carries **one** principle as a `principle` block: the name, two or three sentences on what it means **for this company**, and the source it comes from. The principle frames the lesson; the rest of the lesson shows it on real data. Pick the one that fits the company best, not always the first listed.

## The principles

**Beachhead market.** Win one narrow segment completely before widening. A small market you can dominate gives you references, word of mouth and a repeatable pitch; a broad one spreads you thin. *Source: Geoffrey Moore, Crossing the Chasm.* Use it when the course picks one segment over several.

**Anti-ICP.** Say who you will **not** sell to, and why. It saves more time than any list, and it's where most of the dropped accounts in lesson 2 come from. *Source: common B2B practice.*

**Jobs to be done.** Buyers "hire" a product to make progress on a specific job, and describe it in their own words. Use the customer's words from case studies, not the product's features. *Source: Clayton Christensen.*

**Positioning against the real alternative.** The competition is often a spreadsheet, a person, or doing nothing. Position against what the buyer does today, then say what only you do. *Source: April Dunford, Obviously Awesome.*

**Tier your accounts.** Not every account deserves the same effort. Sort by fit and by timing: high fit with a live signal gets a personal message now, high fit without one gets watched, low fit gets dropped. Show it with a `matrix` block. *Source: account-based marketing practice.*

**A list is only as good as its checks.** One wrong company in front of a prospect costs more trust than ten missing ones. Show the funnel from candidates to kept. *Source: this course's method.*

**Trigger events.** A company is ready when something changes: a new leader, a hire for the job your product does, funding, an acquisition, a new rule. Reach out on the event, not on your calendar. *Source: trigger-event and signal-based selling.*

**Signals decay.** A hiring post is fresh for about a month; a new leader's review window is about 90 days. Act within the window or lose the reason to write. Show dates on a `timeline`.

**Champion vs economic buyer.** The person who feels the pain (the champion) is rarely the one who signs (the economic buyer). Win the champion, then help them sell it upward. *Source: MEDDIC qualification.*

**Multi-thread every account.** Reach two or three people per account, not one. People leave, miss messages and need allies to say yes.

**Problem before product.** The first message is about their problem, in their words, with proof you looked. The product can wait for the reply.

**One ask per message.** Ask one easy question they can answer in a line. Two asks get zero answers.

**Match the tool to the job.** Use the smallest model that does the job well at volume, and the biggest one where a mistake is expensive. Decide by measurement.

**Systems beat heroics.** A weekly check you never skip beats a heroic push once a quarter. Put the repeatable part on a schedule and spend your time on conversations.

**Founder-led sales first.** Early on, the founder should sell: nobody else can change the product, the pitch and the price in the same call. Hire sales once the pitch works without you. *Source: common early-stage advice.*

**Do things that don't scale.** Hand-pick, hand-write and follow up personally with the first customers. Automate only what you've already done by hand and know works. *Source: Paul Graham.*

**Learn from every loss.** Every "no" and every silent account says something about the ICP or the message. Feed it back into lesson 1 and lesson 5.

## Rules

- One principle per lesson, and make it specific to the company ("For Tennr, the beachhead is…"), never generic.
- Cite the source in the `source` field when there is one. The principle's author is fine to name.
- The principle is a frame, not a lecture: keep the `body` to three sentences at most.
